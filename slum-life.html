A Childhood Shaped by Survival

Slum life is not one story. This page looks at the pressures that stack up on a child, and at how they narrow the world a child can see.

PressuresTwo childhoodsThe limited worldTwo loops

Eight pressures at once

These are drawn from what the chapter shows in Seemapuri. They are a way to organise ideas, not a claim about every slum.

Poverty

Earning enough for the day’s food comes first.

Insecure housing

Homes of mud and tin, with no proof of belonging to the city.

Poor sanitation

The chapter notes there is no proper sewage or drainage.

Limited infrastructure

No running water, and, in Saheb’s story, no nearby school.

Child labour

Children start earning early, often in the same work as their parents.

Limited education

Without school, skills and choices stay limited.

Family financial pressure

Every member’s earnings count in the household budget.

Restricted opportunities

Jobs are found through family and neighbours, not through choice.

Source: Anees Jung, “Lost Spring” (NCERT Flamingo), paraphrased. For verified numbers on slums see the research page.

The point of this comparison is not to say one child is worth more. It is to notice how many things a child in the second column has to give up to get by.

Two loops

When options are limited, a child’s choices follow a pattern. Then look at what a different set of choices could be.

Without support

  1. Home
  2. Work
  3. Survival
  4. Repeat

With opportunity

  1. School
  2. Learning
  3. Skills
  4. Career
  5. Dreams
A simplified model. Real lives are messier. Some children combine school and work, and many families do everything they can to keep a child in class. The loops show how limited choices can narrow things down. They do not describe every child, and they do not say the child or the family did anything wrong.
