Anees Jung, the Observer

She does not tell us what to think about Saheb and Mukesh. She shows us what she saw, and lets the questions come from there.

The author

Anees Jung was an Indian author and journalist who wrote columns for newspapers in India and abroad. Much of her work is built from conversations, and many of her books look at the lives of people who are usually not heard, especially women and children.

Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood was published by Penguin Books in 2005. The chapter in our textbook is an extract from it.

Through her lens

A good piece of reportage moves in four steps. Choose each one to see what it looks like in the chapter.

Her point of view, from the text

First-hand

She appears in the chapter as a visitor who talks to Saheb, walks around Seemapuri and enters homes in Firozabad. We see the places as she saw them.

Sympathetic, not sentimental

She notices details, such as Saheb’s worn shoes, without turning the children into symbols of pity.

Attentive to systems

She points to the moneylenders, middlemen and officials in Firozabad as part of the story, not just the families.

Social issues in the chapter

  • Child labour
  • Poverty and debt
  • Occupations that run in families
  • Lack of access to schools
  • Public indifference
I have only described her point of view through things that appear in the text. I am not claiming to know her personal beliefs beyond what she wrote.
Source: Anees Jung, “Lost Spring”, in Flamingo, Class XII English (NCERT, 2007), paraphrased.
