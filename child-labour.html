Childhood or Labour?

Saheb picks rags. Mukesh’s family makes bangles. Both boys are doing work that most of us would say belongs to adults.

DefinitionWhy it happensThe cycleIndia dataThe darker side

What child labour means

Not all work by children counts as child labour. The difference is in what the work does to the child.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) explains that child labour is work that is harmful to a child’s health or development, takes too many hours, or is done by a child who is too young. It usually gets in the way of school and play.

So helping with a family shop for an hour after school is one thing. Sorting garbage all day or working near a glass furnace is another.

In India, the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended in 2016, treats anyone below 14 as a child and prohibits employing them in any occupation, with limited exceptions. It also sets rules for adolescents.

Both Saheb and Mukesh fall in the age group the law is written to protect.

Sources: ILO, “Child labour” topic page (ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/child-labour); Government of India, Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (as amended 2016). Check the Act’s wording before quoting.

Why children end up working

Poverty

When a household is short of money, a child’s earnings can matter more than their schooling.

Family circumstances

In Firozabad, children work in the family trade. In Seemapuri, ragpicking is what everyone does.

Lack of education

Parents who could not study often have few ways to help a child do so, and few other jobs to move into.

Long-term effects on education

The ILO notes that child labour stops children from getting the skills and education they need for decent work as adults.

Economic and social consequences

Fewer skills mean fewer job choices later. At the level of a country, it can mean less trained workers and a slower move out of poverty.

Source: ILO, Fact sheet: Child labour in India (ilo.org/publications/fact-sheet-child-labour-india).

The cycle

One way to picture how it repeats from one generation to the next.

  1. Poverty
  2. Limited education
  3. Child labour
  4. Fewer opportunities
  5. Continued poverty
This is a simplified model. Real situations are more complex. Many children in poor families do go to school, some work and study together, and some families break the pattern with help from a teacher, a scheme or a neighbour. The cycle is a way to think, not a rule.
