Saheb-e-Alam

“Sometimes I Find a Rupee in the Garbage”

AuthorAnees JungSettingSeemapuri, DelhiCharacterSaheb-e-Alam

A boy with a big name

His name means something like “lord of the universe”. His life, as Anees Jung describes it, is anything but grand.

Saheb is a boy who lives in Seemapuri, a settlement on the edge of Delhi where many families of ragpickers live. He goes through the city’s garbage every morning, barefoot, looking for anything that can be sold.

The author is struck by how casually he says things that would shock most of us. For the adults around him, garbage is a way to eat. For Saheb it is also a kind of treasure hunt, because now and then a coin turns up.

The story turns when Saheb shows that he wants to learn. The author says she could start a school, half-seriously, and he takes it at face value. Later she has to admit that her offer had been more of a passing remark than a plan.

By the end he has a “proper” job at a tea stall. He earns a little and gets meals, but the story makes it clear that he has lost something that was, until then, his: his own time.

Saheb’s world in seven cards

Tap a card to open it.

Saheb’s family came from Dhaka. According to the chapter, storms had destroyed their fields and homes, so they left the village and ended up in Delhi.

Ragpicking is what the whole neighbourhood does to earn a living. Children go out with sacks, sort what they find and sell it. Nobody hires them and nobody is answerable to them, so they call it their own work.

The homes in Seemapuri are made of mud with tin or tarpaulin roofs. The chapter mentions the lack of drainage, sewage and running water. Families have ration cards to buy grain, but little that proves they belong to the city.

When the author suggests he should go to school, Saheb says there is none in his area, and he is keen to learn when she says she could start one. The promise is never kept. The chapter treats that as its own small loss.

Saheb is a child, and he works full time. That is child labour: work that takes the place of school and play. The story does not preach about it. It simply shows how ordinary it has become in his neighbourhood.

Near the end, Saheb takes a job at a tea stall. He is paid a monthly amount and given meals. It looks like a step up, since it is steady, but he now works for someone else.

The author notices that he looks less carefree. The sack he used to carry was his own, but the canister he now carries belongs to his employer. The story ends by asking whether a steady wage is worth that trade.

Saheb’s journey

Five turning points, in the order they appear.

  1. Migration

    The family leaves Dhaka after storms and settles in Seemapuri.

  2. Ragpicking

    Every morning starts with the sack and the garbage heaps.

  3. A dream of school

    He hears there might be a school and asks if it is true.

  4. The tea stall

    He finds regular work with an employer.

  5. Loss of freedom

    The work is steadier, but it is no longer his own.

The sack

For Saheb, the sack is what pays for food today. It is heavy every single day.

What if it balanced?

Work and school do not have to be a choice, but for many children they still are.

The school bag

A bag of books is a plan for tomorrow. That is what he asked for.

The sack was his own. The steel canister belongs to someone else.
